What Elite Athletes Can Teach Yoga Teachers

Yoga teachers can borrow from elite performance science by designing attention as carefully as movement. This essay translates quiet eye, cueing, motor prediction, rhythm, recovery, and ecological dynamics into practical teaching principles.

Excellence is rarely about working harder. More often, it is about learning what deserves attention—and what does not.

Watch an Olympic gymnast preparing for a routine.

A Formula One driver moments before the lights go out.

A concert pianist walking onto the stage.

Or an experienced yoga teacher standing quietly before beginning class.

At first glance, these professions appear unrelated.

One demands explosive power.

Another extraordinary precision.

Another artistic expression.

Another contemplative movement.

Yet neuroscience increasingly suggests they depend on remarkably similar principles.

Attention.

Prediction.

Timing.

Adaptation.

The world's highest performers are not simply stronger or more disciplined.

They perceive differently.

The question is not whether yoga resembles elite sport.

The question is whether both disciplines are solving the same neurological problem.

The Quiet Eye

During the 1990s, Professor Joan Vickers began asking a deceptively simple question.

Why do Olympic athletes appear to have more time?

Using eye-tracking technology, she studied elite golfers, basketball players, rifle shooters, surgeons and military personnel.

The expectation was straightforward.

Experts would move their eyes more quickly.

Instead, the opposite happened.

Before initiating movement, elite performers held their gaze remarkably still.

Vickers called this the Quiet Eye.

The final visual fixation before movement consistently lasted longer in experts than in novices.

This quiet period appears to give the brain time to organise the upcoming action before execution begins.

Performance begins before movement.

It begins with attention.

Seeing Before Moving

Quiet Eye research changed how coaches think about skill.

Elite performers are not simply reacting faster.

They are gathering better information before acting.

The same principle appears in yoga.

An experienced practitioner rarely rushes into a balance posture.

Their gaze stabilises.

Their breathing settles.

Weight shifts almost imperceptibly.

Only then does movement begin.

From the outside this looks calm.

From the inside the nervous system is preparing.

Perhaps attention is the first movement.

Everything else follows.

The Brain Builds Internal Models

Professor John Krakauer, one of the world's leading motor neuroscientists, argues that expertise depends on building increasingly accurate internal models.

The nervous system learns not only how to move.

It learns what will happen next.

Experienced climbers recognise holds before touching them.

Elite tennis players begin moving before the ball leaves the opponent's racket.

Professional dancers anticipate the next phrase before the music arrives.

These behaviours are not intuition in the mystical sense.

They are prediction refined through experience.

Movement becomes increasingly proactive rather than reactive.

Efficiency, Not Effort

One of the most replicated findings in sports neuroscience is known as the Neural Efficiency Hypothesis.

Across multiple EEG and functional MRI studies, experienced performers often demonstrate lower cortical activation than novices while achieving superior results.

This does not mean experts use less brain.

It means they use fewer unnecessary resources.

The nervous system becomes economical.

John Kounios observed something similar in expert jazz musicians experiencing flow.

Executive control decreased while sensory and motor systems became more engaged.

Different laboratories.

Different tasks.

The same pattern emerges.

Expertise reduces interference.

Practice Is Becoming Less Repetitive

Traditional coaching assumed mastery required endless repetition.

Modern motor learning has become more sophisticated.

Researchers including Professor Keith Davids have helped develop Ecological Dynamics, an approach arguing that athletes should not simply memorise movements.

They should learn to adapt.

Training therefore becomes richer rather than more repetitive.

Small changes in constraints encourage athletes to discover increasingly effective movement solutions.

The environment becomes the teacher.

This has profound implications for yoga.

Perhaps intelligent sequencing should not merely reinforce familiar movement.

Perhaps it should cultivate adaptability.

What Yoga Teachers Already Do Well

Interestingly, experienced yoga teachers often use principles that sports science only recently formalised.

Progressive challenge.

Variable movement.

External attentional cues.

Breathing as temporal organisation.

Recovery integrated into practice rather than separated from it.

The language differs.

The underlying neuroscience often overlaps.

This does not mean yoga and elite sport are identical.

They pursue different objectives.

One seeks competitive excellence.

The other often seeks self-awareness.

Yet both depend upon the same nervous system.

Coaching Attention Instead of Movement

Perhaps the role of a teacher is changing.

Instead of correcting every joint angle, teachers increasingly become designers of attention.

Where should students look?

When should they breathe?

When should silence replace instruction?

When should effort become exploration?

Elite coaching has gradually shifted from controlling movement toward shaping perception.

Movement follows.

Perhaps yoga teaching is evolving in the same direction.

The Future Coach

Imagine observing a future movement coach.

They understand biomechanics.

Neuroscience.

Motor learning.

Attention.

Environmental design.

Music.

Lighting.

Technology.

Not because each discipline replaces teaching.

But because each offers another tool for shaping experience.

The objective remains unchanged.

Help people move better.

The methods become richer.

Beyond Performance

Elite athletes pursue medals.

Yoga practitioners often pursue something less tangible.

Presence.

Resilience.

Connection.

Self-understanding.

The remarkable discovery of modern neuroscience is that many of the mechanisms supporting these goals appear surprisingly similar.

Attention precedes action.

Prediction supports learning.

Efficiency emerges from expertise.

Environment shapes behaviour.

Perhaps excellence is not a destination unique to athletes.

Perhaps it is a property of every nervous system learning to interact more intelligently with the world.

Research Notes

Joan Vickers — Quiet Eye

Question: What distinguishes elite performers from novices?

Experiment: Eye-tracking studies across golf, basketball, shooting, surgery and military training.

Finding: Experts maintain a longer final fixation before movement, supporting more effective motor planning.

Limitation: Quiet Eye has been demonstrated across many skills, but the precise neural mechanisms remain under investigation.

John Krakauer

Question: How does the brain acquire expertise?

Contribution: Demonstrated that motor learning depends upon developing increasingly accurate internal models through prediction and adaptation.

Keith Davids

Question: How should elite athletes train?

Contribution: Developed Ecological Dynamics, emphasising adaptability over repetition.

Implication: Rich environments often produce more transferable skills than perfectly controlled drills.

Neural Efficiency Research

Finding: Expert performers frequently recruit task-relevant neural networks more efficiently than novices.

Important caveat: Neural efficiency is task-dependent and should not be interpreted as "using less brain."

Open Questions

  • Can Quiet Eye principles improve yoga instruction?
  • Which attentional strategies best support balance and motor learning?
  • Can ecological dynamics inform movement sequencing?
  • How should technology support coaching without replacing human intuition?
  • Is the future movement teacher part neuroscientist, part coach and part environmental designer?

Perhaps the greatest lesson elite sport offers yoga is not how to perform at a higher level.

It is how to create conditions in which better performance emerges naturally.
