Discovery

University research story · 2003

In 2003, university emotion researchers Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt gave awe a sharper psychological shape. Instead of treating awe as a poetic extra, they described it as an encounter with vastness that forces the mind to update its existing frame.

Result: The result separated awe from ordinary pleasure or prettiness. Awe can change scale, attention, meaning and self-focus, while beauty and restoration follow related but different paths. The design implication is powerful, but it must stay humble.

Sources

  • Inner Vision: An Exploration of Art and the Brain

    Semir Zeki · 1999 · Oxford University Press

    Foundational neuroaesthetics book connecting visual-brain specialization with aesthetic experience; useful for explaining why visual form, color, and composition can be treated as perceptual variables rather than decorative extras.

    Source page

  • The Aesthetic Brain: How We Evolved to Desire Beauty and Enjoy Art

    Anjan Chatterjee · 2013 · Oxford University Press

    Synthesizes neuroscience, psychology, and evolutionary arguments about beauty and aesthetic response; useful for keeping beauty claims grounded in empirical aesthetics rather than taste language alone.

    Source page

  • Approaching awe a moral spiritual and aesthetic emotion

    Dacher Keltner; Jonathan Haidt · 2003 · Cognition and Emotion

    Foundational paper defining awe around perceived vastness and a need for accommodation, with moral, spiritual, natural, and aesthetic variants.

    DOI

  • Awe the small self and prosocial behavior

    Paul K. Piff; Pia Dietze; Matthew Feinberg; Daniel M. Stancato; Dacher Keltner · 2015 · Journal of Personality and Social Psychology

    Multi-study paper linking awe experiences with a smaller self-focus and more prosocial tendencies, useful for grounding awe as more than aesthetic pleasure.

    PubMedDOI

  • The Restorative Benefits of Nature: Toward an Integrative Framework

    Stephen Kaplan · 1995 · Journal of Environmental Psychology

    Introduces Attention Restoration Theory as a framework for how environments can help recover directed attention; strong conceptual source for soft fascination and attention fatigue, especially when discussing natural features in designed space.

    DOI

Article Research Notes

Extracted from Why Beauty Matters.

Semir Zeki

Question: Can beauty become a legitimate subject of neuroscience?

Contribution: Founded the field of Neuroaesthetics in 1999.

Finding: Aesthetic experience recruits distributed neural systems involved in perception, reward and meaning rather than a single "beauty centre."

Anjan Chatterjee

Question: How do aesthetic experiences influence cognition?

Contribution: Integrated neuroscience, psychology and philosophy into a modern science of aesthetics.

Finding: Beauty influences attention, emotion, memory and decision-making through interacting neural networks.

Limitation: There is no universal neural signature of beauty; responses depend on context, experience and culture.

Roger Ulrich

Question: Can environmental views influence medical recovery?

Experiment: Compared post-operative hospital patients with windows overlooking trees versus brick walls.

Finding: Patients with views of nature recovered more quickly and required fewer analgesics.

Stephen & Rachel Kaplan

Contribution: Attention Restoration Theory.

Key idea: Natural environments restore directed attention through soft fascination rather than demanding focused effort.
