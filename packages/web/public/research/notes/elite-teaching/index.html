Discovery

University research story · 2016

By 2016, Joan Vickers' Quiet Eye research had turned elite performance into something visible frame by frame. In university sport-science settings, eye tracking showed that experts often hold a longer final fixation before action, as if the body needs one clean moment of visual commitment before movement begins.

Result: The result matters for teaching because expertise is not only strength or repetition. It is attention, timing, feedback and constraint design. Yoga teachers can borrow that lens without pretending that sport findings automatically prove yoga outcomes.

Article Research Notes

Extracted from What Elite Athletes Can Teach Yoga Teachers.

Joan Vickers — Quiet Eye

Question: What distinguishes elite performers from novices?

Experiment: Eye-tracking studies across golf, basketball, shooting, surgery and military training.

Finding: Experts maintain a longer final fixation before movement, supporting more effective motor planning.

Limitation: Quiet Eye has been demonstrated across many skills, but the precise neural mechanisms remain under investigation.

John Krakauer

Question: How does the brain acquire expertise?

Contribution: Demonstrated that motor learning depends upon developing increasingly accurate internal models through prediction and adaptation.

Keith Davids

Question: How should elite athletes train?

Contribution: Developed Ecological Dynamics, emphasising adaptability over repetition.

Implication: Rich environments often produce more transferable skills than perfectly controlled drills.

Neural Efficiency Research

Finding: Expert performers frequently recruit task-relevant neural networks more efficiently than novices.

Important caveat: Neural efficiency is task-dependent and should not be interpreted as "using less brain."
