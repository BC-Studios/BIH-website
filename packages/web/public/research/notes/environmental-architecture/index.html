Discovery

University research story · 1984

In 1984, a university-led environmental psychology question moved quietly into a hospital ward. Roger Ulrich looked at recovery records and noticed that the view outside a patient's window could become part of the medical story: trees on one side, brick wall on the other, the same vulnerable days after surgery.

Result: Patients with tree views had shorter stays and needed fewer strong analgesics. The result did not prove that beauty cures illness; it showed something more precise and more useful for Sansara: environment can participate in attention, stress, recovery and state.

Sources

  • View Through a Window May Influence Recovery from Surgery

    Roger S. Ulrich · 1984 · Science

    Retrospective matched study of postsurgical recovery records comparing patients with tree views against patients facing a brick wall; useful as a landmark source for restorative views, but narrow enough that claims should stay specific to recovery context.

    PubMedDOI

  • The Restorative Benefits of Nature: Toward an Integrative Framework

    Stephen Kaplan · 1995 · Journal of Environmental Psychology

    Introduces Attention Restoration Theory as a framework for how environments can help recover directed attention; strong conceptual source for soft fascination and attention fatigue, especially when discussing natural features in designed space.

    DOI

  • The Free-Energy Principle: A Unified Brain Theory?

    Karl Friston · 2010 · Nature Reviews Neuroscience

    Review article proposing free-energy minimisation as a unifying account of perception, action, learning, attention, and salience; useful for framing humans as active prediction systems coupled to their environments.

    PubMedDOI

  • Inner Vision: An Exploration of Art and the Brain

    Semir Zeki · 1999 · Oxford University Press

    Foundational neuroaesthetics book connecting visual-brain specialization with aesthetic experience; useful for explaining why visual form, color, and composition can be treated as perceptual variables rather than decorative extras.

    Source page

  • The Aesthetic Brain: How We Evolved to Desire Beauty and Enjoy Art

    Anjan Chatterjee · 2013 · Oxford University Press

    Synthesizes neuroscience, psychology, and evolutionary arguments about beauty and aesthetic response; useful for keeping beauty claims grounded in empirical aesthetics rather than taste language alone.

    Source page
