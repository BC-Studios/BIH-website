Shared State Design: The Promise, the Problem, and the Middle Path

Shared state design is powerful because groups are already shaped by rhythm, attention, meaning, environment, and social context. Through Boomtown's Observatory and the Sansara studio model, this essay defines the ethical middle path: design conditions with transparency, consent, flexibility, and respect for individual interpretation.

There is a moment in certain rooms when a group stops feeling like a collection of separate individuals.

A yoga class begins as separate bodies on separate mats. A dance floor begins as strangers moving near each other. A festival begins as thousands of private nervous systems arriving with different histories, moods, fears, desires, and expectations.

Then something changes.

The rhythm becomes shared. Attention begins to gather. The room starts breathing in the same direction. People remain separate, but the experience becomes collective.

We usually describe this vaguely.

"The room had energy." "Everyone was connected." "It felt like one body." "There was something in the air."

But what if this is not only atmosphere?

What if it is design?

Not design as control. Not design as forcing people into the same emotion. Design in the older, deeper sense: arranging conditions so something can emerge.

This is the idea behind shared state design.

Shared state design is the practice of shaping the conditions through which groups can coordinate attention, rhythm, emotion, movement, and meaning.

It does not claim to manufacture connection. It does not claim to induce flow. It does not claim that one room, one song, one light setting, or one ritual will affect everyone in the same way.

It begins with a more careful premise:

Human states are always being shaped by environment.

The question is whether we design those environments unconsciously, or with care.

From individual state to shared state

For decades, wellness has treated the individual as the basic unit.

One body. One nervous system. One breath. One goal. One transformation.

This made sense for a while. Modern wellness grew around self-regulation: calming the nervous system, improving performance, building strength, reducing stress, sleeping better, recovering faster, becoming more resilient.

But in real life, people do not regulate only from the inside.

We regulate through other people. Through rhythm. Through rooms. Through light. Through voice. Through social safety. Through shared timing. Through the feeling that the group knows where it is going.

A good class is not only a sequence of movements. A good festival is not only a lineup. A good ritual is not only symbolism. A good performance is not only sound.

These experiences work because they arrange the relationship between body, attention, environment, and group.

This is where state design becomes shared state design.

A yoga studio, a dance floor, a concert, a ceremony, a martial arts room, a choir, a sports team, a protest, and a festival all reveal the same principle:

People can enter states together.

Not identically. Not perfectly. Not mechanically.

But coherently enough that the group becomes part of the experience.

Boomtown as a living laboratory

This is why Boomtown's Observatory is such a useful case study.

Boomtown is already one of the clearest examples of large-scale immersive state design. It is not just a music festival. It is a temporary city built from sound systems, performance, theatre, architecture, crowds, storylines, costume, ritual, exhaustion, anticipation, and release.

For 2026, The Observatory turns that temporary city into a live research environment.

Led by psychologist Dr Martha Newson, Boomtown describes The Observatory as an academic research hub hosting workshops, panels, and live scientific studies across neuroscience, psychology, performance, identity, and collective behaviour.

That matters because laboratories can isolate variables, but festivals reveal how variables collide.

In a lab, you can study rhythm. At a festival, rhythm meets heat, friendship, costume, alcohol, longing, bass, sleep loss, group identity, weather, architecture, and myth.

In a lab, you can study social bonding. At a festival, bonding happens through movement, shared risk, shared discomfort, shared joy, shared navigation, and shared surrender to a temporary world.

In a lab, you can ask what synchrony does. At a festival, synchrony is already everywhere: in walking, dancing, chanting, queuing, cheering, eating, resting, and moving through the same landscape at the same time.

Boomtown is useful because it shows that shared states are not produced by one stimulus.

They emerge from an ecology.

The science of moving together

The strongest research on collective experience does not say that people become one mind.

That would be too simple.

Instead, the science points to something more subtle: under certain conditions, people can become coordinated across movement, physiology, attention, emotion, and sometimes brain activity.

This is not mystical language. It is coordination language.

Research on dance and synchrony suggests that moving together can increase social bonding. Work on rave culture and identity fusion suggests that intense collective experiences can produce lasting cooperation when rhythm, movement, endurance, and shared meaning converge. Team-flow and hyperscanning research suggests that groups performing together may enter patterns of coordination that are not just individual flow states happening in parallel.

But synchrony is not automatically good.

A crowd can synchronise around panic. A cult can synchronise around obedience. A casino can design arousal for extraction. A social media platform can design craving. A military ritual can design submission. A nightclub can design disinhibition.

Shared state design is ethically delicate precisely because it works on the same human systems that make belonging, ritual, awe, and collective joy possible.

The important question is not only:

Can environments shape groups?

They already do.

The important question is:

Who is shaping the group state, by what means, with what consent, and for whose benefit?

The room is not neutral

Every room has a nervous-system effect.

A fluorescent gym creates one kind of body. A candlelit studio creates another. A courtroom creates another. A hospital waiting room creates another. A nightclub creates another. A church creates another. A festival creates another.

The environment does not determine experience, but it participates in it.

Light changes alertness. Music changes pace. Bass changes the body. Rhythm organises timing. Language directs attention. Breath alters arousal. Space changes behaviour. Crowds change perception. Shared movement changes belonging.

This is the hidden architecture of experience.

Most wellness spaces already use this architecture, but often unconsciously.

A teacher chooses a playlist. A studio dims the lights. A facilitator builds intensity. A class peaks, then descends. A breath cue softens the room. A silence lands after music. A chant or shared sound creates group orientation.

These are not decorative choices.

They are state-design choices.

The problem is not that we are designing states.

The problem is pretending that we are not.

Shared state design is not control

The biggest danger in this field is arrogance.

It is tempting to say:

"We create flow." "We induce calm." "We regulate the nervous system." "We engineer transformation." "We synchronise the room."

This language is too strong.

Human beings do not respond like machines.

No two people enter the same class from the same baseline. One person may arrive rested, open, and socially safe. Another may arrive anxious, grieving, traumatised, overstimulated, hormonally shifted, or physically depleted.

The same beat can feel energising to one person and threatening to another.

Darkness can feel liberating to one person and unsafe to another.

Stillness can feel meditative to one person and unbearable to another.

A phrase like "surrender" can feel devotional to one person and coercive to another.

This is why shared state design must never become a fantasy of control.

The designer does not install a state into the participant.

The designer arranges invitations.

The participant remains sovereign.

The four layers of shared state design

A mature model of shared state design has at least four layers.

1. Shared rhythm

Rhythm is not background. It is infrastructure.

A rhythm gives the group a timing system. It tells bodies when to move, when to wait, when to build, when to soften, and when to return.

In music, rhythm allows strangers to coordinate without speaking.

In yoga, rhythm helps breath and movement become predictable.

In dance, rhythm allows individuality and togetherness to coexist.

In festivals, rhythm becomes a public nervous system. Thousands of people move through the same pulse while still having private experiences inside it.

This is why music is not just emotional decoration.

It is temporal architecture.

2. Shared attention

A group becomes more coherent when it knows where attention belongs.

That attention might be on the breath, the beat, the teacher, the body, the visual field, the next transition, or the shared silence after intensity.

A confused room fragments. A coherent room gathers.

This does not mean everyone must think the same thing. It means the experience has enough structure that people are not constantly asking, consciously or unconsciously:

"What is happening now?" "What should I do?" "Am I safe?" "Where are we going?"

Good design reduces unnecessary cognitive negotiation.

It lets people participate more fully.

3. Shared feedback

A strong group experience gives constant feedback.

The teacher's voice gives feedback. The music gives feedback. The lighting gives feedback. The sequence gives feedback. The room's energy gives feedback. The people around you give feedback.

In a badly designed environment, feedback is noisy. The body has to work too hard to interpret the experience.

In a well-designed environment, the cues are coherent.

The room tells you where you are in the arc.

Arrival. Orientation. Build. Peak. Descent. Stillness. Return.

This does not remove mystery.

It creates enough safety for mystery to happen.

4. Shared meaning

People do not only respond to stimuli.

They respond to meaning.

A drumbeat is not only sound. A blue light is not only wavelength. A chant is not only vibration. A posture is not only biomechanics. A festival stage is not only architecture.

Everything arrives carrying association, memory, culture, identity, and story.

This is why shared state design must be semantically careful.

Words like tribe, warrior, surrender, feminine, masculine, divine, mother, discipline, release, and transformation can be powerful. They can also be loaded, manipulative, or exclusionary.

A mature designer does not assume universal meaning.

They leave room for interpretation.

The ethical edge

Shared state design becomes dangerous when it hides its intention.

If a space is designed to calm people, say so.

If a class is designed to build intensity, say so.

If a ritual is designed to invite emotional release, say so.

If a festival study is collecting data, consent must be clear.

If wearable technology or biometric feedback is being used, participants must know what is measured, why it is measured, how it is stored, and whether they can opt out.

The stronger the state, the stronger the ethics need to be.

There are five basic principles:

Consent. People should know what kind of experience they are entering.

Choice. People must be able to step back, modify, observe, or leave.

Transparency. The design intention should not be hidden.

Non-certainty. No facilitator should promise the same outcome for everyone.

Return. A class should not only take people somewhere. It should bring them back with more agency than they had before.

The return matters.

A powerful state without return can leave people open, scattered, dependent, or confused.

A well-designed state arc includes integration.

It gives people a way back to themselves.

What this means for wellness

The future of wellness will not only ask:

"What exercise are we doing?"

It will ask:

"What state are we entering?" "What state are we practising?" "What state are we leaving people in?" "What happens when people practise that state together?"

This does not mean every class needs to become technical or overdesigned.

It means we become more honest about what great teachers, artists, performers, and ritual leaders have always known:

The room matters.

The timing matters. The music matters. The silence matters. The transition matters. The lighting matters. The beginning matters. The ending matters. The group matters.

A class is not only content.

It is an ecology.

Sansara and the shared state system

This is where Sansara sits.

Sansara is not trying to control consciousness.

It is not trying to make every person feel the same thing.

It is not claiming that music, lighting, sequencing, breath, or sensory design can manufacture flow.

Instead, Sansara begins from a more careful idea:

The class is already shaping state. So let us shape it with more care.

A teacher is already building a journey.

Arrival. Orientation. Warm-up. Rhythm. Build. Peak. Descent. Stillness. Return.

Sansara helps bring the layers of that journey into relationship.

The sequence is not separate from the music. The music is not separate from the breath. The breath is not separate from the lighting. The lighting is not separate from the emotional arc. The emotional arc is not separate from the group.

Sansara treats the studio as a shared state system: a place where movement, sound, light, breath, language, and attention can become coherent enough for people to participate more deeply.

The teacher remains the creator.

Technology supports.

The student stays sovereign.

That is the middle path.

The future is not individual optimisation

The wellness industry has spent years optimising individuals.

Better sleep. Better recovery. Better focus. Better fitness. Better nervous-system regulation.

All of that still matters.

But the next frontier is not only individual optimisation.

It is collective coherence.

How do we design rooms where people feel safe enough to move? How do we create rhythm without coercion? How do we build intensity without overwhelm? How do we invite connection without forcing intimacy? How do we use technology without replacing human sensitivity? How do we support altered states without pretending to control them? How do we bring people back with more agency, not less?

These are the questions of shared state design.

Boomtown's Observatory matters because it recognises that some of the most powerful human experiences do not happen in isolated laboratory conditions. They happen in the wild: in temporary cities, on dance floors, in crowds, in rituals, in moments where strangers become briefly coordinated by sound, story, and movement.

Sansara brings the same question into the studio.

Not:

"How do we make people feel something?"

But:

"How do we design the conditions in which a more coherent shared experience can emerge?"

That is the real promise.

Not control.

Not manipulation.

Not technological mysticism.

But care.

The room is already speaking to the nervous system.

Shared state design simply asks us to become responsible for what it is saying.
