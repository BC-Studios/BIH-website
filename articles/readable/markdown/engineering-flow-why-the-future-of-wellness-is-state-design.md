# Engineering Flow: Why the Future of Wellness Is State Design

For decades, fitness has focused on a simple equation: more repetitions, more weight, more calories burned.

Neuroscience is pointing in a different direction.

Human performance depends not only on the body we train, but also on the state in which that training occurs.

A distracted brain and an immersed brain can perform the same movement while having fundamentally different experiences.

This shift has given rise to a new question.

Rather than asking, *How do we design better workouts?*

We should ask, *How do we design better states?*

Flow researcher Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi first described flow as a state in which action and awareness merge, self-consciousness fades and performance feels effortless.

For many years, scientists explained flow primarily as a balance between challenge and skill.

Recent neuroscience suggests the picture is more nuanced.

In 2024, David Rosen and colleagues at Drexel University recorded the brain activity of 32 jazz musicians while they improvised. Their results suggested that the highest-flow performances were associated with reduced activity in executive-control regions of the frontal cortex alongside increased engagement of sensory and motor networks.

Professor John Kounios concluded that productive flow appears to emerge from two ingredients: years of deliberate practice and the ability to withdraw conscious control once expertise has been acquired.

The implication reaches far beyond music.

Great experiences are not built by demanding more conscious effort.

They are built by allowing well-trained systems to operate with less unnecessary interference.

At Sansara, we think this principle extends beyond movement.

Music, lighting, sequencing and environmental design cannot manufacture flow.

What they can do is reduce friction, organise attention and create conditions in which flow becomes more likely.

The future of wellness may not be measured by how intensely we exercise.

It may be measured by how intelligently we design the states in which we move.

**Key references**

• Csikszentmihalyi M. *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience.*

• Rosen DS et al. (2024). Neural correlates of flow during jazz improvisation.

• Kounios J. Drexel University Center for Cognitive Neuroscience.
