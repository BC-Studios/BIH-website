# Can We Measure Flow Like We Measure Heart Rate?

## The next frontier in performance science may be measuring the quality of experience itself.

Heart rate is easy to measure.

Flow is not.

A watch can estimate sleep stages, recovery, respiration, skin temperature and training load. A cyclist can measure power output in watts. A runner can track pace, cadence and lactate threshold. Elite teams can quantify fatigue with increasing precision.

Yet one of the most important performance states remains largely invisible.

Flow.

The condition in which attention narrows, self-consciousness fades, action feels fluid and time appears to change.

Athletes describe it.

Musicians describe it.

Surgeons describe it.

Dancers describe it.

Yoga practitioners describe it.

The problem is not whether flow exists as a subjective experience.

The problem is whether we can measure it without destroying it.

---

## Flow Began As A Reported Experience

Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi did not discover flow inside a scanner.

He discovered it through interviews.

Artists, climbers, athletes and chess players repeatedly described a similar state of deep absorption. The details varied, but the structure remained consistent.

Clear goals.

Immediate feedback.

High concentration.

Reduced self-consciousness.

Altered sense of time.

A feeling that action and awareness had merged.

Flow began as phenomenology.

A science of first-person experience.

That origin matters.

Before flow became a neuroscience problem, it was a human description.

Any attempt to measure flow must preserve that fact.

---

## The Measurement Problem

Most flow research still depends on questionnaires.

Researchers ask participants how absorbed they felt.

How much control they experienced.

Whether time changed.

Whether the activity felt intrinsically rewarding.

These tools are useful.

They are also limited.

They interrupt the experience.

They depend on memory.

They compress a dynamic state into a score.

They are vulnerable to expectation, personality and language.

Flow may fluctuate second by second.

Questionnaires usually capture what remains after the experience has already ended.

It is like measuring music by asking someone to remember the rhythm afterwards.

Useful.

Incomplete.

---

## The Search For Neural Markers

In 2024, David Rosen, John Kounios and colleagues brought flow into a more precise experimental setting.

They recorded EEG from thirty-two jazz guitarists while the musicians improvised.

After each performance, musicians rated their own flow. Independent experts also evaluated the quality and creativity of the improvisations.

The findings suggested that high-flow moments in experienced musicians were associated with reduced activity in parts of the executive-control network and increased engagement of sensory and motor regions.

This result matters because it challenges a common misconception.

Flow does not appear to be simple hyperfocus.

It may involve expertise becoming fluid enough that excessive conscious control can recede.

But this does not give us a universal “flow biomarker.”

The study examined expert jazz musicians.

Not yoga practitioners.

Not runners.

Not groups.

Not beginners.

The finding is powerful precisely because it is specific.

---

## Why One Biomarker Is Unlikely

Performance culture often wants a single number.

A flow score.

A readiness score.

A recovery score.

A consciousness score.

The nervous system is rarely that simple.

Flow likely involves multiple interacting systems:

attention,

reward,

motor control,

prediction,

interoception,

autonomic regulation,

emotion,

self-referential processing.

Different activities may produce different neural signatures.

A pianist in flow, a climber in flow and a yoga teacher in flow may share psychological features while relying on different sensorimotor systems.

This means flow may not have one biological fingerprint.

It may have a pattern.

---

## What We Might Measure Instead

Perhaps the better question is not:

“Can we measure flow directly?”

But:

“Can we measure the conditions that make flow more likely?”

That is a more scientifically grounded approach.

Possible signals include:

heart rate variability,

respiratory rhythm,

movement smoothness,

gaze stability,

pupil dilation,

electrodermal activity,

EEG patterns,

facial tension,

cadence stability,

synchrony with music,

synchrony with others,

reduction in unnecessary movement variability.

No single signal proves flow.

But together, they may estimate immersion, attention and state transition.

This is how modern health technology already works.

Sleep trackers do not observe sleep directly.

They infer it from patterns.

Flow measurement may follow the same path.

---

## The Quiet Eye

Professor Joan Vickers’ work on elite athletes offers a useful analogy.

Using eye-tracking, she discovered that experts often maintain a longer final visual fixation before movement. She called this the Quiet Eye.

The Quiet Eye is not flow.

But it demonstrates something important.

An invisible performance state can sometimes leave measurable behavioural traces.

Attention becomes visible through the eyes.

In a movement studio, similar signals may exist.

Where do participants look before balance?

How stable is gaze during challenge?

Does visual attention become quieter as skill improves?

These questions are not yet standard in yoga research.

They should be.

---

## Flow In Groups

Individual flow is difficult to measure.

Collective flow is harder.

A group experience may involve synchrony across multiple people:

movement timing,

breathing rhythm,

heart rate dynamics,

vocal patterns,

posture,

attention,

emotional expression.

Researchers studying interpersonal synchrony have already shown that humans can align behaviour and physiology during interaction.

The next question is whether immersive movement environments can increase this alignment.

A future studio may not only measure individual states.

It may measure the coherence of the room.

Not to rank people.

Not to surveil them.

But to understand whether the environment is supporting shared attention.

---

## The Ethical Problem

Measuring experience is powerful.

It is also dangerous.

If flow becomes another metric, it can be misused.

Teachers may optimise scores instead of students.

Studios may gamify presence.

Companies may turn inner life into performance data.

This would miss the point.

The goal should not be to force people into measurable states.

The goal should be to understand how environments support or interrupt human experience.

Some of the most important aspects of practice may remain unmeasurable.

Meaning.

Trust.

Safety.

Beauty.

Belonging.

A good science of flow must leave room for what cannot yet be quantified.

---

## From Quantified Body To Quantified Experience

The first wave of wearables measured the body.

Steps.

Calories.

Heart rate.

Sleep.

The next wave may measure interaction.

How movement relates to rhythm.

How breath relates to attention.

How a group synchronises.

How environments influence recovery.

This is a different paradigm.

Not quantified self.

Quantified relationship.

The unit of analysis becomes the system:

person,

movement,

music,

space,

teacher,

group,

time.

Flow may be the first truly ecological performance metric.

---

## The Future Studio

Imagine a future movement studio that does not ask participants how they felt after every class.

Instead, it quietly studies the environment.

Was the music aligned with movement?

Were transitions smooth?

Did the room synchronise during peak sequences?

Did recovery actually reduce physiological arousal?

Did participants move with greater continuity over time?

The aim is not to label a class as successful or unsuccessful.

It is to learn.

To refine.

To reduce environmental friction.

To design better conditions for human experience.
