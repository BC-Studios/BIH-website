# Can Light Become a Performance Variable?

## We optimise nutrition to the gram. Why do we still treat light as decoration?

Elite athletes track sleep with remarkable precision.

They measure heart rate variability, resting heart rate, glucose responses, training load and recovery scores. Teams invest millions into biomechanics, nutrition and physiology.

Yet many still train under the same lighting used in office buildings.

For centuries, light was considered little more than a practical necessity. It allowed us to see.

Modern neuroscience tells a very different story.

Light is one of the brain's most powerful biological signals.

Long before it helps us perceive the world, it helps regulate how the brain prepares to interact with it.

The question is no longer whether light affects performance.

The question is whether we have underestimated just how deeply it shapes human physiology.

---

## The Discovery That Changed Circadian Biology

Until the early 2000s, scientists believed the eye contained only two major photoreceptors.

Rods.

Cones.

One for dim light.

One for colour.

Then, in 2002, neuroscientist Professor David Berson and colleagues identified a third class of retinal cells known as **intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs)**.

Unlike rods and cones, these cells are not primarily concerned with vision.

Instead, they measure ambient light and communicate directly with brain regions responsible for biological timing.

Their photopigment—melanopsin—responds particularly strongly to short-wavelength blue light.

This discovery transformed circadian neuroscience.

The eye was no longer simply a camera.

It was also a biological sensor regulating the body's internal clock.

---

## Light Talks Directly to the Brain

The signals generated by melanopsin-containing cells travel to the **suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** within the hypothalamus.

Often called the body's master clock, the SCN coordinates daily rhythms across almost every organ system.

Sleep.

Hormones.

Body temperature.

Alertness.

Metabolism.

Immune function.

Rather than asking, "What time is it?" the SCN asks, "How much light is reaching the retina?"

Professor Russell Foster, whose work transformed modern circadian biology, has repeatedly argued that humans remain profoundly dependent on natural light-dark cycles despite living in technologically advanced societies.

Artificial lighting has extended our productive hours.

It has also fundamentally altered one of the oldest biological signals guiding human physiology.

---

## Performance Begins Before Movement

We often imagine performance beginning with the first repetition, the first sprint or the first yoga pose.

Neuroscience suggests it begins much earlier.

The nervous system continuously adjusts its readiness according to environmental cues.

Morning light promotes alertness and helps synchronise circadian timing.

Evening light delays melatonin secretion and can shift sleep timing.

These effects are no longer controversial.

The challenge is understanding how they translate into movement performance.

Researchers studying athletes increasingly investigate how light exposure influences:

* reaction time

* sleep quality

* mood

* perceived energy

* recovery

* cognitive performance

The strongest evidence currently supports light's indirect effects through circadian regulation and sleep.

The evidence for acute improvements in physical performance from specific lighting conditions is promising but still developing.

This distinction matters.

Scientific credibility depends as much on recognising uncertainty as celebrating discovery.

---

## Colour Is Not the Story

Walk through the wellness industry and you will quickly encounter confident claims.

Blue light energises.

Red light calms.

Purple enhances spirituality.

Green improves creativity.

The reality is considerably more nuanced.

Current neuroscience does not support assigning simple emotional properties to individual colours.

The brain does not contain a "blue relaxation circuit" or a "red motivation centre."

Instead, responses depend on multiple interacting variables:

* intensity

* timing

* duration

* spectral composition

* personal history

* culture

* expectations

* surrounding context

Colour psychology remains an active field of research, but many commercial claims extend well beyond available evidence.

This is important because oversimplification prevents genuine innovation.

The future of lighting will not emerge from colour myths.

It will emerge from systems thinking.

---

## Dynamic Light Instead of Static Light

Nature rarely provides constant illumination.

Morning gradually brightens.

Clouds move.

Sunset changes colour temperature minute by minute.

Fire flickers.

Moonlight shifts with weather.

Modern buildings, by contrast, often produce static environments.

The same intensity.

The same spectrum.

The same brightness.

Hour after hour.

Circadian researchers such as Professor Satchin Panda have argued that the timing of light exposure is at least as important as the amount of light itself.

This raises an intriguing possibility.

Perhaps movement environments should also become dynamic.

Not because dynamic lighting is inherently superior.

But because biological systems evolved within dynamic environments.

The nervous system expects change.

---

## Beyond Visibility

Lighting has traditionally been designed to maximise visibility.

The future may require a broader objective.

Supporting cognition.

Supporting recovery.

Supporting attention.

Supporting rhythm.

Imagine entering a movement studio where illumination gradually evolves throughout the class.

The opening encourages orientation.

The main practice enhances environmental clarity without visual overload.

Recovery becomes progressively softer.

The participant may never consciously notice the transitions.

That may be precisely the point.

Great environmental design rarely demands attention.

It quietly shapes experience.

---

## Light as Part of a Larger System

One of the most common mistakes in performance science is studying variables in isolation.

Light.

Music.

Temperature.

Architecture.

Breathing.

Each becomes its own discipline.

The nervous system experiences none of them separately.

Every movement unfolds within an integrated sensory landscape.

A participant entering a studio does not perceive light independently of rhythm.

Nor rhythm independently of social context.

Nor social context independently of space.

Experience is multisensory by definition.

Perhaps this explains why some environments immediately feel coherent while others never quite do.

The individual components may be excellent.

The system is not.

---

## From Lighting Design to State Design

The purpose of future lighting systems may not be to illuminate rooms.

It may be to support human states.

Not by manipulating behaviour.

Not by forcing emotion.

But by aligning environmental signals with what the nervous system is already trying to accomplish.

Preparation.

Focus.

Learning.

Recovery.

Connection.

Flow.

Lighting becomes one instrument in a larger orchestra.

Its value emerges not from acting alone, but from working in harmony with architecture, movement, rhythm and human biology.

---

## Looking Forward

Every major advance in performance science has changed the questions we ask.

We moved from asking how to build stronger muscles to how to improve recovery.

Then from recovery to sleep.

Then from sleep to circadian biology.

Perhaps the next question is even broader.

Not how we optimise people.

But how we optimise the environments in which people perform.

If the twentieth century taught us how to train the body, and the early twenty-first century taught us how to measure physiology, the decades ahead may teach us something equally important:

How to design light not simply for seeing—

but for living, learning and moving well.
