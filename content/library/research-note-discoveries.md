# Research Note Discoveries

Editable discovery-story source for `/research/notes/` pages. Each note is rendered into the public research note as a short cinematic discovery and a result.

## note-environmental-architecture
- Year: 1984
- Discovery: In 1984, a university-led environmental psychology question moved quietly into a hospital ward. Roger Ulrich looked at recovery records and noticed that the view outside a patient's window could become part of the medical story: trees on one side, brick wall on the other, the same vulnerable days after surgery.
- Result: Patients with tree views had shorter stays and needed fewer strong analgesics. The result did not prove that beauty cures illness; it showed something more precise and more useful for Sansara: environment can participate in attention, stress, recovery and state.

## note-light-performance
- Year: 2002
- Discovery: In 2002, university vision-science labs began to make light feel less like ambience and more like physiology. Researchers studying retinal ganglion cells showed that some light responses were not about seeing an image at all; they were about setting time inside the body.
- Result: The finding helped establish that light reaches circadian and non-image-forming systems through specialized pathways. Light became a biological signal, not only a visual condition, which is why lighting can be treated as a performance variable with real constraints.

## note-rhythm-entrainment
- Year: 1996
- Discovery: In 1996, a university music-and-neurology research setting turned rhythm into a clinical instrument. Michael Thaut and colleagues placed external rhythmic cues beside Parkinsonian gait, asking whether sound could lend timing to a nervous system struggling to organise movement.
- Result: Rhythmic auditory stimulation improved gait measures such as timing, stride and cadence in the studied population. The larger discovery is not that music is magic; it is that rhythm can become a motor scaffold when the cue, task and nervous system are matched.

## note-predictive-brain
- Year: 1867
- Discovery: In 1867, Hermann von Helmholtz stood at the border of physiology, optics and philosophy and treated perception like a mystery solved under pressure. The eye received fragments, yet the mind somehow produced a world. More than a century later, university neuroscience would formalise that intuition through predictive processing and active inference.
- Result: The result is a shift in how movement environments are understood: the brain does not passively receive a room, a cue or a body signal. It predicts, tests, corrects and acts. A yoga studio is therefore a prediction environment, not just a physical space.

## note-sensory-coherence
- Year: 2008
- Discovery: In 2008, university neuroscience returned to a deceptively simple problem: what happens when sight, sound, touch and timing arrive together? Barry Stein and Terrence Stanford reviewed how the nervous system binds signals across senses, and how timing or spatial mismatch can change the result.
- Result: The result gives sensory coherence its scientific spine. The brain is not processing separate design features one by one; it is trying to assemble a coherent cause. When light, sound, breath, voice and movement compete, attention pays the cost.

## note-flow-state
- Year: 1988
- Discovery: In 1988, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi brought years of interviews and psychological observation into the language of optimal experience. Across artists, athletes and skilled workers, he listened for the same signature: total absorption, clear goals, immediate feedback and a challenge that met ability at the edge.
- Result: Flow became a describable state rather than a vague high. Later neuroscience and performance studies added texture, but the core result remains practical: immersion is more likely when skill, challenge, feedback and attention are arranged with care.

## note-creative-insight
- Year: 2003
- Discovery: In 2003, university cognitive psychology labs began catching the Aha moment before it fully arrived in consciousness. Edward Bowden and Mark Beeman studied insight problems and showed that a solution could be partially active before a person could explain how they knew.
- Result: The result made creative insight less mystical without making it mechanical. Ideas can emerge from preparation, unconscious activation and reduced interference. For Sansara, this supports the link between environment, attention, flow and creative release.

## note-state-engineering
- Year: 1979
- Discovery: In 1979, James Gibson reframed perception from a university psychology tradition into something more alive: the world is perceived through possibilities for action. Decades later, predictive regulation and distributed cognition extended that idea across body, tools, people and environments.
- Result: The result is the logic behind state engineering. A room is not a backdrop, and a practice is not only an exercise prescription. Conditions can be arranged so attention, action and regulation become easier to find.

## note-elite-teaching
- Year: 2016
- Discovery: By 2016, Joan Vickers' Quiet Eye research had turned elite performance into something visible frame by frame. In university sport-science settings, eye tracking showed that experts often hold a longer final fixation before action, as if the body needs one clean moment of visual commitment before movement begins.
- Result: The result matters for teaching because expertise is not only strength or repetition. It is attention, timing, feedback and constraint design. Yoga teachers can borrow that lens without pretending that sport findings automatically prove yoga outcomes.

## note-flow-measurement
- Year: 2024
- Discovery: In 2024, a university neuroscience study placed jazz guitarists under measurement while they improvised. David Rosen, John Kounios and colleagues watched creative flow through EEG, comparing expert and non-expert performance as the musicians moved from effort into absorption.
- Result: The result suggested that flow can be triangulated through expertise, task context, self-report and neural dynamics. It is not a universal flow meter, but it points toward a future where experience is measured carefully instead of guessed from vibe alone.

## note-breath-regulation
- Year: 1878
- Discovery: In 1878, Claude Bernard's physiology lectures placed the body's internal environment at the centre of life. The drama was quiet but radical: survival depends not only on what happens outside the body, but on how internal conditions are regulated moment by moment.
- Result: That lineage leads toward homeostasis, allostasis, interoception and yoga self-regulation. Breath becomes meaningful because it sits at the threshold between voluntary action and bodily regulation, where attention can meet physiology.

## note-awe-beauty
- Year: 2003
- Discovery: In 2003, university emotion researchers Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt gave awe a sharper psychological shape. Instead of treating awe as a poetic extra, they described it as an encounter with vastness that forces the mind to update its existing frame.
- Result: The result separated awe from ordinary pleasure or prettiness. Awe can change scale, attention, meaning and self-focus, while beauty and restoration follow related but different paths. The design implication is powerful, but it must stay humble.

## note-invisible-tech
- Year: 2000
- Discovery: In 2000, university HCI researchers James Hollan, Edwin Hutchins and David Kirsh argued that cognition does not live only inside the head. It spreads across tools, representations, people and the environment, especially when a system is designed well enough to carry part of the work.
- Result: The result reframes invisible technology. A good interface is not invisible because it disappears physically; it becomes less mentally expensive. In a movement environment, technology should lower friction rather than ask for more attention.

## note-teacher-workload
- Year: 1988
- Discovery: In 1988, John Sweller's cognitive-load research made a hidden cost visible: learning can be harmed when working memory is spent on unnecessary complexity. In a studio, the teacher faces a related burden, holding sequence, timing, music, bodies, safety and atmosphere in mind at once.
- Result: The result gives teacher workload a research spine without overclaiming. Teaching is invisible cognitive labour. Better systems should reduce sequencing and attentional load so the teacher can return to observation, presence and practice.
